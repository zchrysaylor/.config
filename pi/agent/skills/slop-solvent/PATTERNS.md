# Patterns to detect and fix

Read alongside SKILL.md. Numbers are stable identifiers local to this skill. Retired rules leave gaps. Examples illustrate patterns, not unconditional bans on their component words.

## Content

1. **Inflated significance and legacy.** Cut claims that something "marks a pivotal moment", "reflects broader trends", "sets the stage", or leaves an "indelible mark" unless the text supports a specific consequence. Ordinary facts do not need an attached claim about cultural importance, community identity, or ecological value.

2. **Promotional description.** Replace "nestled in the heart of", "rich cultural heritage", "breathtaking", "renowned", "groundbreaking", and similar advertising with relevant facts. Preserve supported distinctions instead of turning them into generic praise.

3. **Superficial participle clauses.** Inspect trailing "highlighting", "underscoring", "ensuring", "reflecting", "symbolizing", "contributing to", "showcasing", and "fostering" clauses. Delete empty commentary. If a clause makes a meaningful claim, retain it only with appropriate support. Keep literal actions such as "The worker exits, releasing the lock" when accurate.

4. **Vague attribution and invented consensus.** "Experts believe", "industry reports suggest", "observers have cited", and "some critics argue" need identifiable sources. Attribute an opinion to its actual source. Do not turn one critic into "critics" or imply an exhaustive list has additional members. Flag unsupported substantive claims.

5. **Coverage substituted for substance.** Do not list "independent media outlets", "leading experts", an "active social media presence", or "awards and recognition" merely to assert importance. State the relevant finding, award, or reported event. Keep attribution where it matters; cut self-certification of notability and source quality.

6. **Vague relationships.** Replace "associated with", "connected to", and "in connection with" with the known relationship, such as "taught at", "founded", or "cited". Do not infer a role that the source does not establish.

7. **Formulaic challenges and future prospects.** Remove the automatic arc "Despite its success, X faces challenges... Despite these challenges, the future is bright." Retain actual limitations, supported plans, and unresolved questions. Do not add optimistic forecasts or a concluding section just to complete an outline.

8. **Article-about-the-title openings.** Define the subject rather than treating "List of X" or a title's disambiguator as a proper name. "Catchment area (health) refers to..." becomes a definition of a health facility's catchment area. Remove duplicate titles supplied by the publishing system.

9. **Feelings instead of behavior.** "SQL you can read", "types that follow your schema", and "the database stays close at hand" omit the useful fact. Describe the documented action, interface, or result. Ask whether the sentence could be pasted unchanged into another project's docs. If so, make it specific using available facts or cut the empty claim.

10. **Unsupported inference and bias.** Do not infer importance, causation, motives, consensus, privacy preferences, or missing history from thin evidence. A failed search does not establish that information is unavailable. Watch for softened criticism, one-sided praise, and unsupported institutional narratives. Flag substantive problems rather than polishing them into credible-sounding assertions.

## Rhetorical tics

11. **Stock negative contrasts.** Rewrite "not just X, but Y", "not only X, but also Y", "it's not X, it's Y", and "Y rather than X" when they manufacture a misconception or grand reveal. State Y directly, or state both facts. Preserve a real distinction the reader needs, such as "This flag deletes local files, not remote files."

12. **Negation chains.** Collapse two or more "No X, no Y" or "did not X, did not Y" / "didn't X, didn't Y" items used as a slogan. Retain distinct exclusions or actions that matter, expressed plainly. "No server, no account" can become "It runs locally without an account" if supported.

13. **Repeated imperative reversal.** "Don't call it X. Call it Y." and other "Don't VERB it... VERB it" patterns stage a correction. Give the accurate name or instruction, with a reason when necessary.

14. **Whole/entire declarations.** Inspect "That's the whole point/game/thing", "X is the whole trick/pitch/idea", "here is the whole...", "X is the entire point/game/business model", and "the entire point/game/business model is...". Remove the totalizing wrapper and explain the claim. Do not inflate one factor into the sole explanation.

15. **Scripted reflection and validation.** Remove stock "sit with that/this/it", "sit with the discomfort", "you already know", "worth naming", "that's not nothing", and "this/it/which is not nothing" phrasing. Replace it with a relevant observation or action. Preserve appropriate empathy without instructing readers how to feel or claiming to know their thoughts.

16. **Declarations that something is real.** "The X is real, and...", "The X is real, not...", and "is the real... and it..." often supply validation without detail. State the actual condition and consequence. Do not flag literal terms such as "real estate", "real time", or "real numbers" on this basis.

17. **Performative honesty.** Cut "I won't pretend", "I'll be honest", "let's be honest", and introductory "Honestly", "Look", or "to be clear" when they only advertise sincerity. Make the clarification itself. Keep literal discussion of honesty or an actual disclosure.

18. **Gesturing at a favored detail.** "That's the part...", "the part that makes me trust the rest", and "my favourite part of..." often replace an explanation. Name the detail and why it matters. Keep a requested personal preference only if it belongs to the writer, not an invented narrator.

19. **Narrowing superlative reveals.** "The only X I trust", "the only thing it needs", and "the only X that matters" need justification. State the relevant requirement or preference without fabricated exclusivity. Preserve supported factual uniqueness.

20. **Stock verification invitations.** Replace "don't take my word for it" and "you don't have to take my word for any of this" with the actual source, command, or reproducible check. Never claim the check was run unless it was.

21. **Staged revelations.** Cut "turns out", "it turns out that", "here's the twist/thing/catch/kicker/rub", "here's the first example", and "the punchline is/:/?" when they add only suspense. State the result, limitation, or example directly. Preserve meaningful chronology when a discovery itself matters.

22. **Simplicity slogans.** "Fits in your head", "small enough to hold in your head", "batteries included", "it just works", "zero config", and "sane defaults" need concrete descriptions. Name included dependencies, required setup, default values, or relevant size if known. Do not invent numbers or guarantees.

23. **Obituaries and retroactive importance.** Replace "X is dead; long live Y", "that's why X mattered", and "this is why preserving Y mattered" with the actual change or causal explanation. Keep literal deaths and documented discontinuations.

24. **Stranded auxiliary contrasts.** "The tool died; the data didn't" and "Reading passed. Writing didn't" create a rehearsed reversal. Make the result explicit, such as "Reading passed the tests, but writing failed." Do not guess the missing predicate in an ambiguous "Maybe it wouldn't have."

25. **Echoing sentence runs.** Merge adjacent sentences with the same skeleton, such as "A shopping cart is an object in the system. A chat room is an object in the system." Do not substitute different synonyms for the repeated predicate just to conceal the pattern.

26. **Repeated openers.** Inspect three or more consecutive sentences starting with the same meaningful word, such as "Maybe... Maybe... Maybe...". Combine related points or restructure around their content. Repeated pronouns and articles alone are not a problem. Preserve purposeful parallel instructions when useful.

27. **Stacked rhetorical questions.** Two or more questions in sequence, often fragments, can dramatize a point instead of explaining it. Answer the questions or state the decision criteria. Keep genuine questions in interviews, questionnaires, and requests for missing information.

28. **Forced triples and colon-led enumerations.** Do not pad ideas into three adjectives, benefits, or examples. Inspect a colon followed by three or more comma-separated items when it supplies only an impression of detail. This is a weak signal in technical documentation; leave factual enumerations alone by default. Use the natural number of items.

## Language and readability

29. **Watchlist vocabulary.** Inspect the companion vocabulary lists for clusters, vague adjectives, and metaphorical uses. Prefer ordinary words or the concrete mechanism. Do not replace every match mechanically, especially precise technical or literal uses.

30. **Elaborate substitutes for basic verbs.** Replace "serves as", "stands as", "functions as", and "represents" with "is" when they add no distinction. Replace "boasts", "features", "offers", and "maintains" with "has" when possession is all that is meant. Preserve actions and temporary roles that differ from identity.

31. **Synonym cycling.** Pick one name for a person or concept. Do not alternate "protagonist", "main character", "central figure", and "hero" to avoid repetition. Preserve genuinely different concepts and domain distinctions.

32. **False ranges.** "From X to Y" needs a meaningful scale, progression, or range. If X and Y are just unrelated topics, list them directly. Keep real chronological, numerical, geographic, or conceptual ranges.

33. **Filler and transitions.** Shorten "in order to" to "to", "due to the fact that" to "because", and "in the event that" to "if". Delete "it is important to note that" and similar throat-clearing. Remove automatic "moreover", "furthermore", "additionally", "ultimately", "in summary", and repeated section recaps when the connection is already clear.

34. **Excessive hedging and intensifiers.** Reduce "could potentially possibly be argued that it might" to the appropriate qualification, often "may". Preserve evidence-based uncertainty, frequency, and scope. Do not turn "may reduce" into "reduces" or "some" into "all".

35. **Weak verbs propped up by adverbs.** Inspect "significantly improves", "works seamlessly", "quietly transforms", "genuinely helps", and "runs quickly". State the action or a supplied measurement. Do not manufacture benchmarks or remove an adverb that changes the claim, such as "partially" or "intentionally".

36. **Dense sentences and unnecessary passive voice.** Split sentences that require backtracking. Prefer one main idea per sentence. Name the actor when known: "The loader parses the file." Passive voice is fine when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or less important than the result. Do not mistake every "is + participle" for an editing error.

37. **Mannered metaphors and fragments.** Replace "a dial worth turning", "wire it or delete it", "rides along", and "stands on" with their literal meaning. Avoid aphorisms, personified code, theatrical fragments, and slogans when plain description would work.

38. **Over-compression.** Restore articles and verbs. Replace arrow chains, unexplained abbreviations, and compressed notes with sentences when they force decoding. Keep notation where the format calls for it, such as a diagram or command synopsis. Do not replace verbose slop with telegraphic slop.

## Style and communication

39. **Punctuation as emphasis machinery.** Apply the main skill's dash and colon defaults. Avoid replacing every dash with the same new punctuation pattern. Use straight quotes by default outside protected content; preserve required typography and valid notation.

40. **Excessive formatting.** Remove decorative emojis, arbitrary bold emphasis, and horizontal rules between every section. Use sentence-case headings. Repair skipped heading levels, duplicate top-level titles, and empty heading hierarchies when unnecessary. Keep navigation structures that serve the document.

41. **Redundant inline headers and tables.** "**Performance:** Performance improved..." repeats its label. Use prose or give the actual result. A useful lead-in such as "**Schema in TypeScript.** Tables live in one file" is fine. Keep tables for real comparisons, not two facts that fit in a sentence.

42. **Chatbot residue and flattery.** Cut "Of course!", "Certainly!", "Great question!", "You're absolutely right!", "Found the smoking gun!", "I hope this helps", and automatic "Let me know if..." closers. Remove drafting advice accidentally pasted into finished prose. Keep substantive questions and necessary coordination.

43. **Boilerplate caveats and incomplete drafts.** Remove irrelevant "as an AI language model" or training-cutoff framing from the deliverable, without hiding relevant limitations. Replace broad claims about missing information with the actual scope of a search if known. Flag unfilled names, dates, URLs, and abrupt truncations; do not complete them with guesses. Preserve placeholders in an intentionally reusable template.

44. **Self-congratulatory summaries.** Avoid claiming to have "ensured clarity, neutrality, and compliance", "retained all key information", or "addressed reviewer feedback" instead of naming the edit. Report the actual change. Mention preserved behavior, parameters, tests, or policy requirements when they are relevant, not as an exhaustive ritual.

45. **Artificial voice changes.** Match the surrounding register and English variety. Preserve useful individuality instead of imposing generic corporate prose. Do not introduce errors, fake experiences, slang, or arbitrary sentence-length changes to appear human.

## Markup and source integrity

46. **Wrong output markup.** Use the destination's syntax. Markdown is appropriate in Markdown documents and usually inappropriate inside a Wikipedia article. Repair mixed Markdown/wikitext, broken lists, and malformed templates only when their intended form is established. Do not invent categories, infoboxes, template parameters, review states, or maintenance banners.

47. **Leaked tool artifacts.** Inspect `contentReference`, `oaicite`, `oai_citation`, `turn0search0`, `attributableIndex`, stray `+1` source counters, `[cite: 1]`, `[span_1](start_span)`, `grok_card` / `grok-card`, `grok_render_citation_card_json`, lenticular-bracket citations, `[attached_file:1]`, `[web:1]`, `ppl-ai-file-upload` links, and `:::writing` wrappers. In exported prose, resolve them to real references where possible. Never strip the only evidence marker and leave the claim looking verified. Preserve valid citation syntax in a system that renders it, and literal examples discussing the artifacts.

48. **Citation defects.** Flag broken links, invalid ISBNs, unresolved or mismatched DOIs, missing page locators, unsupported quotations, unused reference definitions, and undefined named references. A working URL or valid checksum does not prove that a source supports the sentence. Do not fabricate repairs, remove citations as cosmetic clutter, or claim verification without reading the source. Keep original access dates unless correcting a known error.

49. **Tracking and irrelevant reference links.** Remove tracking-only `utm_source=chatgpt.com`, `utm_source=openai`, `utm_source=copilot.com`, or `referrer=grok.com` parameters when cleaning reference URLs and their removal does not affect the resource. Preserve meaningful query parameters, anchors, access tokens, and signed URLs. A tracking parameter is not evidence that the surrounding prose was generated by AI.
