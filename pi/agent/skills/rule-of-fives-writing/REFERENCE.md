# Rule of Fives Reference

This skill distills Arthur F. McEvoy's Rule of Fives method into practical drafting and revision instructions for paragraphs, essays, and longer arguments.

## Core idea

Effective expository writing makes one point and proves it in a manageable number of logical steps. The canonical shape uses five moves:

1. Introduction: orient the reader and state the thesis.
2. Body I: prove the first step.
3. Body II: prove the second step.
4. Body III: prove the third step.
5. Conclusion: finish the argument and show why it matters.

Three body steps usually give the reader enough structure to remember the argument without making it unstable or diffuse. The number five comes from adding an introduction and conclusion to those three steps.

## Key terms

- **Topic**: the subject area. Example: “remote work.” A topic is not enough.
- **Thesis**: the point to prove, stated in one declarative sentence with an active verb. Example: “Hybrid work improves retention when managers redesign meetings, feedback, and promotion norms.”
- **Argument**: the ordered sequence of reasons that proves the thesis.
- **Analysis**: breaking the subject into parts and explaining how those parts relate.
- **Clincher**: the final sentence of a paragraph that signals closure, restates the point, links back to the thesis, or transitions forward.

## Thesis checklist

A strong thesis should:

- State one main idea, not several competing ideas.
- Use a concrete subject and active verb when possible.
- Make an arguable claim rather than announce an intention.
- Be specific enough to control the whole piece.
- Invite a reader to think, “Really? Show me.”

Weak: “This essay will discuss remote work and productivity.”

Stronger: “Hybrid work improves productivity only when managers replace office visibility with explicit goals, deliberate communication, and fair evaluation.”

## Style constraints

- Avoid formulaic contrast frames such as “It is not X; it is Y,” “not only X but also Y,” and “not by X but by Y.”
- State the positive claim directly, then explain the contrast only if the argument requires it.
- Never use em dashes. Use commas, parentheses, colons, semicolons, or separate sentences instead.

## Five-sentence paragraph template

Use this strict form when drafting, teaching, or repairing unclear prose:

1. **Topic sentence**: state the paragraph's point in one active, declarative sentence.
2. **Development 1**: give the first reason, fact, example, or analytical move.
3. **Development 2**: give the second reason, fact, example, or analytical move.
4. **Development 3**: give the third reason, fact, example, or analytical move.
5. **Clincher**: close the point, connect it to the larger thesis, or transition to the next paragraph.

The middle three sentences may use conjunctions or subordinate clauses to express complex ideas, but the paragraph should still make only one point.

## Five-part essay template

### Introduction

- Orient the reader to time, setting, issue, or problem.
- State the problem or tension that makes the topic worth reading.
- State the thesis in one sentence with an active verb.
- Optionally preview the three steps of the argument.

### Body I, II, III

Each body paragraph or section should:

- Begin with a topic sentence that states one step in the argument.
- Develop that step with evidence, examples, explanation, or analysis.
- End with a clincher or transition.
- Support the thesis directly.

Common sequences:

- Cause → mechanism → consequence
- Background → problem → solution
- Claim → objection → answer
- Similarity → difference → significance
- Past → present → future
- Principle → application → implication

Avoid three unrelated examples unless the thesis claims that the examples collectively prove a single pattern.

### Conclusion

A conclusion should:

- Signal completion.
- Recap the three steps without sounding mechanical.
- Restate the thesis in fresh language.
- Move the reader back into the wider world by answering “so what?”

## Test paragraph method

Before expanding an essay, compress it into five sentences:

1. Main thesis.
2. Body I topic sentence.
3. Body II topic sentence.
4. Body III topic sentence.
5. Conclusion topic sentence or final claim.

Read those five sentences as a paragraph. If they do not track, the essay will not track. Fix the outline before drafting prose.

A good test paragraph should:

- Move in a visible order.
- Avoid repeating the same claim five ways.
- Avoid sudden shifts in scope, audience, or terms.
- Prove the thesis rather than merely list related facts.

## Rewriting diagnostic

When rewriting user-provided text, check these issues first:

1. **No thesis**: the piece has a subject but no arguable point.
2. **Multiple theses**: the piece tries to prove several unrelated claims.
3. **Passive or vague thesis**: the central sentence hides the actor or action.
4. **List structure**: the body paragraphs sit side by side but do not build an argument.
5. **Paragraph drift**: a paragraph starts with one idea and ends with another.
6. **Unsupported assertions**: claims need evidence or examples.
7. **Weak conclusion**: the piece stops rather than finishes.

Then rewrite by creating a thesis, choosing three steps, running the test paragraph, and expanding only the material that serves the argument.

## Scaling beyond five paragraphs

For longer essays or papers, apply the same structure recursively:

- The whole paper has an introduction, three main sections, and a conclusion.
- Each main section has its own mini-thesis, three subpoints, and section conclusion.
- Each paragraph has one topic sentence, focused development, and a clincher.

Allocate space before drafting. For example, a 15-page paper might use 2 pages for the introduction, 4 pages for each of three sections, and 1 page for the conclusion.

## Rewrite output pattern

For substantial rewrites, use this format unless the user asks otherwise:

```md
## Diagnosis
- Thesis: ...
- Main structural issue: ...
- Best Rule-of-Fives structure: ...

## Test paragraph
...

## Rewrite
...

## Notes
- ...
```

For small rewrites, provide only the revised paragraph plus a one-sentence note.

## Draft output pattern

For new writing from a topic, use this format unless the user asks for only the final draft:

```md
## Thesis
...

## Outline
Introduction: ...
I. ...
II. ...
III. ...
Conclusion: ...

## Draft
...
```

## Mini example

Topic: “students using phones in class”

Thesis: “Classroom phone bans improve learning when schools pair them with clear exceptions, consistent enforcement, and better in-class engagement.”

Test paragraph:

Classroom phone bans improve learning when schools pair them with clear exceptions, consistent enforcement, and better in-class engagement. Clear exceptions protect legitimate needs such as medical monitoring, translation, and family emergencies. Consistent enforcement prevents the ban from becoming a daily negotiation between teachers and students. Better in-class engagement addresses the boredom that makes phones tempting in the first place. A good phone policy therefore succeeds when it treats attention as a schoolwide habit rather than as a device problem alone.
